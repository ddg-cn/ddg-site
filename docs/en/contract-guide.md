# Responsible Contracting

**How the contract signed at the start decides what happens to workers later**

*Short guide, edition 1, November 2026. For buyers (brands, importers, trading companies) and supplier factories, especially small and medium-sized manufacturers in China. Part of the series: stage 2 of the [business relationship lifecycle](/en/ddg#_0-8-where-each-guide-fits-the-life-of-a-business-relationship).*

::: tip In one minute
1. **Most supply contracts are one-way.** The supplier warrants full compliance with the buyer's code; the buyer promises nothing about price, lead times or changes. Model clauses now exist to fix this.
2. **Write the buyer's duties in.** Price that covers legal wages, realistic lead times, written order changes, payment for cancelled work, and no retroactive discounts.
3. **Payment: 60 days or less, agreed before the order.** Chinese law sets 60 days as the default for large buyers paying SMEs, and bans "pay when paid".
4. **List every penalty at the start**, cap it at actual loss, and make the buyer's late payment a breach too.
5. **Accept equivalent audits** instead of repeating them, and say who pays.
6. **Plan the way out now.** A responsible exit clause that binds whichever side ends the relationship costs nothing to sign and a great deal to lack.
:::

## 1. Why the contract matters

A buyer's code of conduct is usually attached to the contract as a list of things the supplier must guarantee. The American Bar Association working group that drafted the most widely used model clauses described this warranty approach as "unrealistic and ineffective": it turns due diligence into box-ticking, and it says nothing about the buyer's own role in causing problems.

That role is real. Prices below cost, short lead times, late changes and cancellations push factories into excessive overtime, unauthorised subcontracting and unpaid wages:

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| ILO and ETI global supplier survey (1,454 suppliers, 87 countries) | Only **17%** of suppliers said they had enough lead time |
| *Abandoned?* (316 Bangladeshi suppliers, March 2020) | When orders were cancelled, **72.1%** of buyers refused to pay for materials already bought and **91.3%** refused to pay production costs |
| University of Aberdeen and Transform Trade (about 1,000 Bangladeshi factories, 2023) | **20%** were paid less than the contract price; **25%** were paid more than three months after delivery |
| Better Buying Purchasing Practices Index 2025 (1,360 suppliers) | Only **43.6%** had payment terms of 60 days or less; suppliers in China more often reported terms of **91 days or more** |

A contract that writes down both sides' duties is the cheapest place to prevent these problems. It is also where the end of the relationship is decided: the [Responsible Exit Guide](/en/exit-guide) shows how much harder an exit is when the contract is silent on notice, payment for work in progress and tooling.

## 2. The model clauses to start from

You don't need to draft from scratch. Three sets of model clauses cover most of what a fair supply contract needs.

| Model | Year | Written for | What it adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ABA Model Contract Clauses 2.0** (MCCs 2.0), with **Schedule Q**, the Responsible Purchasing Code of Conduct for buyers | 2021 | Buyers | Due diligence by both sides instead of one-way warranties (§1.1); buyer duties on price, change orders and assistance (§1.3); a right to cure and a remediation plan before termination (§2.3–2.4); indemnities reduced where the buyer's breach contributed (§6.5(b)) |
| **Responsible Contracting Project, Supplier Model Contract Clauses 1.0** (SMCs) | 2023 | Suppliers (drafted with a consortium of manufacturers) | The most concrete buyer duties: forecasting (3.2), paid reserved capacity (3.3), capped samples (3.4), cancellations treated as order changes (3.9), penalties listed at the outset and capped at actual loss (3.12), acceptance of equivalent third-party audits (3.13), responsible exit by either party (9) |
| **European Model Clauses** (EMCs) | Zero draft 2024; version 1.0 expected late 2026 | Both, for EU due-diligence law | Shared responsibility, information sharing, complaints, remediation, responsible purchasing and exit. Check responsiblecontracting.org for the final text. |

Two more are useful for specific clauses:
- **ICC Force Majeure and Hardship Clauses (2020)**, including a short form suited to SMEs; either party may terminate if the impediment lasts more than 120 days.
- **The Chancery Lane Project** climate clauses, for carbon and environmental terms. Pair them with cost-sharing terms; pushing climate duties onto a small supplier without data or money is the same one-way problem.

The European Commission must adopt guidance on voluntary model clauses under the amended CSDDD by **26 July 2027** (Art. 18).

**Who uses them.** C&A has rewritten its supply terms using the SMCs, with labour costs ring-fenced under ACT's labour costing protocol. Ganni, HAKRO and the B Corp standards also draw on them. Zeeman publishes a two-way code of conduct and pays suppliers 14 days after shipment.

## 3. What to write in: the commercial terms

The six areas where contracts most often go wrong, with the responsible version and where it comes from.

| Term | Responsible version | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Covers all production costs, including legal wages, benefits and health and safety. Labour cost shown separately where possible. Price reviewed when minimum wages, materials or currency move sharply. | Schedule Q 3.3, 4.6; SMC 3.6, 3.8; CFRPP principle 5; ACT |
| **Payment** | Agreed before the order; 60 days or less; no retroactive changes; deposits or shorter terms for small suppliers | Better Buying; CFRPP principle 4; Schedule Q 4.10 |
| **Forecasts and capacity** | Shared early and explained; binding only where agreed; reserved capacity paid for | SMC 3.2–3.3; CFRPP principle 3 |
| **Order changes** | In writing; price and deadline adjusted; the supplier may refuse changes that would cause excessive hours, without retaliation | Schedule Q 4.1–4.4; SMC 3.9; ABA §1.3(d)–(e) |
| **Cancellations** | Treated as an order change: finished goods, work in progress and materials bought are paid for | SMC 3.9; COVID-19 experience |
| **Penalties and chargebacks** | All listed at the outset; none added later; capped at actual loss; evidence given; the buyer's late payment is a breach too | SMC 3.12; CFRPP principle 4 |

**What COVID-19 taught.** In March 2020 many buyers cancelled finished and in-process orders and cited force majeure. Primark first stopped orders, then committed GBP 370 million for goods in production; H&M, adidas, Inditex and PVH also committed to pay. Others did not. Where payment for work in progress was written into the contract, suppliers had a claim; where it was left to goodwill, they waited for campaigns.

## 4. Audits, data and remedies

- **Accept equivalent audits.** SMC 3.13 lets the supplier offer a recent equivalent third-party audit or shared assessment, which the buyer accepts unless it falls short of minimum standards. Repeating audits costs factories time and money and adds nothing.
- **Say who pays.** Under the amended CSDDD (Art. 10(5)), where a company seeks contractual assurances from an SME partner, the terms must be "fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory", and the company bears the cost of independent third-party verification. Omnibus I also limits information requests: partners with fewer than 5,000 employees may be asked only for what cannot reasonably be obtained another way (new Art. 8(2a)).
- **Handle worker data lawfully.** Audits collect names, IDs, wages and interview notes. Sending them abroad needs a lawful route under China's Personal Information Protection Law (Arts. 38–39) and, in most cases, separate consent. Write into the contract what is collected, how it is minimised or anonymised, where it is stored and who receives it.
- **Remediate before terminating.** A right to cure and a remediation plan (ABA §2.3–2.4) protect workers from the harm of a sudden exit. Keep immediate termination for zero-tolerance issues only.
- **Name the grievance channel.** Say how the supplier's workers can raise problems, including problems caused by the buyer's practices. See [Grievance Channels](/en/grievance-guide).

## 5. For the factory: Chinese law when you sign

| Topic | Rule | Practical point |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard terms** (格式条款) | The drafter must draw attention to terms that limit its liability; if it doesn't, the other party can argue the term is not part of the contract. Terms that unreasonably exclude the drafter's liability or restrict the other party's main rights are void; ambiguous terms are read against the drafter. *(Civil Code Arts. 496–498)* | These protect you only **if Chinese law governs**. Check the governing-law clause before relying on them. |
| **Liquidated damages** | A court or arbitral tribunal can reduce damages that are excessively higher than the loss *(Art. 585)* | Still negotiate them down at signing; reduction is discretionary. |
| **Force majeure** | Notify promptly and provide proof within a reasonable time; no excuse if you were already late *(Art. 590)* | Keep records the day it happens. |
| **Change of circumstances** | An unforeseeable, major change making performance clearly unfair allows renegotiation, then modification or termination by a court or tribunal *(Art. 533)* | Not a substitute for a price-review clause. |
| **Payment by large buyers** | A large enterprise buying from an SME pays within 60 days of delivery unless a reasonable different term is agreed; no "pay when paid"; no forced acceptance of commercial bills *(Regulation on Ensuring Payment to SMEs, Order 802, Arts. 9, 11; from 1 June 2025)* | Tell the buyer you are an SME when contracting (Art. 3). The rule clearly reaches buyers' sourcing entities in China; whether it reaches a buyer contracting from abroad is uncertain. |
| **Disputes** | China's revised Arbitration Law took effect on 1 March 2026 | Agree a route: negotiation, then mediation, then arbitration or court. |

**Sanctions clauses.** Many buyer contracts say the buyer may stop paying if the supplier becomes subject to sanctions. Acting on such a clause against a Chinese party can expose the buyer to suits under the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law (Art. 12) and the 2026 regulation on improper foreign extraterritorial jurisdiction (Order 835). In 2024 a Chinese engineering firm that had been put on a US sanctions list sued a foreign customer that stopped paying; the Nanjing Maritime Court froze the customer's vessels and the case was settled. A safer clause provides for **notice, consultation and payment for goods already delivered**, within whatever the sanctions allow. See [Responsible Exit, chapter 9](/en/exit-guide#_9-situation-5-exits-forced-by-sanctions-import-bans-and-conflicting-laws).

## 6. What the law requires in contracts

Less than people think. No law reviewed prescribes specific clauses.

| Law | Contract-related duty |
|---|---|
| **EU CSDDD** (as amended, applies from July 2029) | Seek contractual assurances from direct partners, with verification (Art. 10(2)(b), 10(5)); adapt the company's own purchasing practices (Art. 10(2)(d)); support SME partners (Art. 10(2)(e)). Assurances alone do not prove compliance, and must not shift the company's duties onto suppliers. |
| **Germany, LkSG** §6(4) | Human-rights and environmental expectations in supplier selection; contractual assurances, cascaded along the chain; training; contractual control mechanisms such as audits |
| **China** | Civil Code rules on standard terms and damages; Order 802 on payment to SMEs |

The legal minimum is a code-of-conduct clause. The responsible version is the two-way contract described above.
